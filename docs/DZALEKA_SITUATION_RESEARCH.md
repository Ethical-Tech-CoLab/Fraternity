## 2. Pathway A — Legal & policy

Malawi's encampment policy rests on its reservation to **Article 26 of the 1951 Refugee Convention** (freedom of movement), treated as non-binding. Domestic law is the **Refugees Act, 1989**, which sets status-determination procedure but does not enumerate refugee rights. [Dzaleka encyclopedia](https://services.dzaleka.com/encyclopedia/law-and-encampment-policy)

**Correction, Sept 2026:** Malawi's encampment policy rests on reservations to the 1951 Convention that number **nine in total**, not a single Article 26 reservation — per Dzaleka.com's own 2025 year-in-review reporting, the Aug 2025 National Implementation Plan (NIP) on Migration explicitly targets lifting all nine, giving the domestic reform track a broader scope than this file previously described. [dzaleka.com, 2025 Year in Review](https://www.dzaleka.com/2025/12/dzaleka-refugee-camp-2025-year-in-review.html)

**Update, Sept 2026 — bill timeline:** Malawi's Parliament was briefed by WFP/UNHCR that the Law Commission's review "is expected to be concluded by August 2026" (per Parliament of Malawi's own account) — a firmer but later marker than the original "bill expected in Parliament 2026." **Updated 5 Oct 2026:** the 9 October 2026 publication date for the draft Refugee Bill is now reported by several civil-society sources (Inua Advocacy's director; Dzaleka Online; EU statement urging Parliament to consider the Bill), though still not confirmed on an official Law Commission channel. Inua Advocacy has published a 7-point checklist for assessing it: rights, asylum procedures, institutional powers, protection, freedom and livelihoods, durable solutions, accountability. [Parliament of Malawi](https://www.facebook.com/ParliamentOfMalawi/videos/wfp-and-unhcr-meet-the-speaker-of-parliament/1027595889853599); [Innocent Magambi (Inua Advocacy)](https://www.facebook.com/i.magambi/posts/the-malawi-law-commission-has-announced-that-on-9-october-it-will-make-public-th/1666187755508264); [Dzaleka Online](https://www.dzaleka.com/2026/09/inua-advocacy-calls-for-work-business.html)

**Reading:** Malawi is pursuing two parallel, seemingly contradictory tracks — a domestic legislative review it controls, and a refusal of the same substance as a binding UN-level commitment. This looks like a sovereignty distinction, not simple inaction.

**Financial consequence, newly identified:** Malawi is excluded from the World Bank's **International Development Association (IDA) Window for Host Communities and Refugees** (WHR; formerly the Refugee Sub-Window, RSW) — a concessional financing facility ($2.4B under IDA20; $5.4B approved across 100 operations as of Dec 2025). **21 countries are eligible as of Dec 2025**, including three of Dzaleka's own origin countries (Burundi, DRC, Rwanda) and neighbouring **Zambia** ([ReliefWeb / World Bank, Dec 2025](https://reliefweb.int/report/bangladesh/21-countries-now-eligible-ida-financing-support-refugees-and-hosts)). Eligibility requires hosting at least 25,000 refugees (Malawi qualifies), an "adequate framework for the protection of refugees", and a strategy for long-term solutions ([IDA](https://ida.worldbank.org/en/replenishments/ida19-replenishment/windows-host-communities-refugees)). Malawi's reservations likely fail the protection-framework test (audit 5 Oct 2026): the earlier "14 other countries" figure was outdated. Not confirmed that Malawi applied and was rejected — this is a strong inference, not a stated fact.

**Decongestion instrument tied to this pathway:** Kayilizi Open Settlement, Chitipa District — corrected figures from UNHCR's own Malawi strategy page (Sept 2026): **439 hectares** (166.5 ha for agriculture/irrigation; the previously reported 330 ha figure appears to have been an underestimate), \~689km from Lilongwe, \~15km from the Zambian border. Estimated cost **US$85.2 million** (US$ = United States dollars) (revised from the earlier $90M figure, though local reporting as recently as Jun 2026 still cites a "$90 million gap"). Site planning, land acquisition, and the environmental and social impact assessment (ESIA) are now reported complete — a step forward from "stuck at financing stage," though the funding gap itself is not yet confirmed closed. [UNHCR Malawi](https://www.unhcr.org/us/where-we-work/countries/malawi)

**Updated 5 Oct 2026 — relocation has not started.** The Commissioner for Refugees describes the site as "about 145 hectares in Chitipa", which could be irrigated. Relocation has not begun because the government is still mobilising resources to compensate the landholders; UNHCR's representative confirmed that compensation is incomplete. That conflicts with the "land acquisition complete" reading above, and the 145 ha figure conflicts with UNHCR's 439 ha. Both are kept, not reconciled. [Nation Online, Sept 2026](https://mwnation.com/japan-invests-in-dzaleka-refugees-resilience)

### 3.5 Context and causes

**USAID dismantling, Dzaleka-specific:** per ProPublica-obtained State Dept cables, WFP's Malawi country representative warned in advance that the funding collapse would trigger border closures, protests at UN/WFP offices, and food-aid suspension — before these happened. A named State Dept official (Jeremy Lewin) ordered WFP grant terminations across 14+ countries in Apr 2025 (independently confirmed by Associated Press (AP)/Reuters/American Broadcasting Company (ABC)/Public Broadcasting Service (PBS)) — but **Malawi is not on that named list**, and is also absent from the list of countries whose grants were later reinstated (Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Jordan, Ecuador, Somalia). Dzaleka's own funding collapse (UNHCR-attributed, Jun 2025) looks like a separate, later event, not a direct consequence of the Lewin cuts.

**Current WFP ration status (updated 5 Oct 2026): blanket assistance has ended.** Local press reported rations "cut to zero" in Jun 2026, but WFP's Jul 2026 brief confirmed rations were still at 60% then, with the cut framed for September 2026. That has now happened. UNHCR Malawi's representative (Precious Nkoka) said September 2026 marks **the end of WFP's blanket food assistance** to refugees; from then on support is *targeted* at the most vulnerable (families with children, people with disabilities). The Commissioner for Refugees (Ignacio Maulana) called September "the last month" of WFP support: "the situation is bad. And it's going to get worse." How many refugees the targeted scheme excludes has not been published. [Nation Online, Sept 2026](https://mwnation.com/japan-invests-in-dzaleka-refugees-resilience); [Malawi Broadcasting Corporation (MBC), Sept 2026](https://mbc.mw/japan-funds-biofertiliser-production-for-300-refugee-women-at-dzaleka)

**Documented causal chain:** funding cut → UNHCR downsizing (guards/supervisors laid off) → non-governmental organization (NGO) withdrawal (Plan International exited entirely, Oct 2025 — [The New Humanitarian, Oct 2025](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/opinion/first-person/2025/10/20/remember-we-exist-refugees-malawi-aid-slashed)) → protection-service vacuum → deteriorating protection outcomes (Pathway D).

**New compounding pressures, Sept 2026 refresh:** WFP's Jul 2026 brief warns of forecasted El Niño impacts for the 2026/2027 season, threatening crop production nationally (the "80%+ probability of a strong El Niño" figure appears only in the [services.dzaleka.com summary](https://services.dzaleka.com/resources/wfp-malawi-brief-2026-july) of that brief, not in the WFP text checked on 5 Oct 2026) — echoing the dynamic behind the 4,000-household parametric-insurance payout cited in Pathway C. Separately, over 38,000 Malawians have returned from South Africa following anti-foreigner attacks there (as of Jul 2026, per the [services.dzaleka.com summary of WFP's Jul 2026 brief](https://services.dzaleka.com/resources/wfp-malawi-brief-2026-july) ⚠️), straining the same national reception/social-protection systems Dzaleka depends on, though this is a distinct population from Dzaleka's refugees. Dzaleka's ration crisis sits inside a much larger national WFP funding gap (§3.4) that also threatens school meals for \~594,000 Malawian children from Sept 2026. On the financing side, the World Bank approved a $165.9M national grant (the total grant for the whole project, not an annual amount; the project duration is not stated in the source) (Integrated Social Protection for Resilience and Opportunity Project, INSPIRE-O, 17 Sept 2026; [World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/09/17/malawi-world-bank-grant-financing-to-improve-resilience-to-climate)) for climate/economic-shock resilience and social protection — not refugee-specific and not confirmed to reach Dzaleka, but the first concessional financing at this scale to reach Malawi since this research flagged its World Bank IDA host-refugee-window exclusion (§2).

## 4. Pathway C — Disaster risk & institutional capacity

| Hazard | Assessment | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flood | Credible, elevated — seasonal (Nov–Apr) heavy rains | Terrain/digital terrain model (DTM) studies exist; no public floodplain delineation |
| Fire | High likelihood/impact — dense shelter layout, flammable materials | Documented 2023–2024 fire incidents; UNHCR after-action report |
| Disease (cholera, malaria) | Actively surveilled, background risk | Not currently an acute outbreak per latest sampling |

**The evacuation-command gap is a real capability gap, not just a documentation gap.** The Multi-Hazard Early Warning System platform (MHEWC.org) states district-level Department of Disaster Management Affairs (DoDMA) still *needs to build* — not just publish — camp-level contingency plans, early-warning triggers, and evacuation protocols. Malawi does have a **National Multi-Hazard Contingency Plan (NMHCP) 2025–26** (DoDMA-coordinated, 11 clusters including Shelter/Camp Coordination and Camp Management (CCCM) and Protection, ~$82M funding gap, rains expected to start late, around mid-Dec). Whether it names Dzaleka or refugees has not been checked; the plan itself has not been read (updated 5 Oct 2026; [Africa Brief summary](https://africabrief.substack.com/p/malawi-prepares-multi-hazard-contingency)). Referral capacity: Dowa District Hospital (\~10km), tertiary referral Lilongwe (\~45km); emergency services rated "critical" — limited ambulances, no night access.

Dowa District was on the affected-councils list for Malawi's Jan–Mar 2026 floods; camp-specific damage at Dzaleka is not yet confirmed either way. **Cyclone Freddy (Mar 2023) impact on Dzaleka is refuted**, not just unconfirmed — a contemporaneous statement says directly: "Freddy has not had any impact on refugees in Dzaleka camp" (Dowa fell outside all 15 officially affected southern districts).

**Water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) data exists** (correcting an earlier read that it didn't): water access, latrine ratios, and hygiene-promoter ratios are documented across three time periods. A 2024 malnutrition baseline exists (stunting 38%, wasting 1%) — but it predates the 2025–26 ration cuts, so the nutritional impact of the funding collapse remains undocumented; no 2025/2026 mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) or global acute malnutrition (GAM) data was found.

One risk-financing precedent: UNHCR Malawi used the African Risk Capacity (ARC) Group's "Replica" programme (with Germany's KfW) to insure against drought. After the El Niño drought it received a payout of **about $408,000**, which paid **4,000 refugee and host households about $33 per month for three months** ([VOA](https://www.voanews.com/a/malawi-refugees-receive-first-ever-insurance-payout-/7872490.html); UNHCR Southern Africa Operational Update) (audit 5 Oct 2026): the earlier wording omitted the three-month duration and the total.

**National flood context confirmed, not yet Dzaleka-specific, Sept 2026:** Malawi's March 2026 floods affected 368,000 people (81,842 households) across 23 district and city councils, causing 37 deaths, 233 injuries, and displacing 26,213 people into 84 evacuation camps (Dept. of Disaster Management Affairs, via WFP Malawi Country Brief, Apr 2026). Dowa District's specific inclusion and any Dzaleka-specific damage remain unconfirmed — the same gap flagged in the original research. A published flood-risk model specific to Dzaleka does exist in the academic literature (a remote-sensing/hydrology study combining surface-runoff and exceedance-probability layers), corroborating the "terrain/DTM studies exist" finding above, though it does not appear to have been operationalized into a public evacuation product.

**GBV is a recurring structural mechanism, not a one-off 2025–26 shock.** The identical WFP-ration-cut → survival-sex pattern occurred in 2015–16 too, independently named by WFP's own country director, a community leader, Luxembourg's Deputy Prime Minister, and Plan International staff. Plan International's Oct 2025 exit removed the only actor holding child protection, sexual and gender-based violence (SGBV), and human rights under one mandate; no source shows who absorbed that caseload. No camp-wide GBV prevalence survey exists, and no confirmed integrated referral center (One-Stop Centre) serves Dzaleka.

**Broader human-rights context:** INUA Advocacy, an independent camp monitor, was expelled from Dzaleka in Nov 2023 — likely retaliatory. The Centre for Human Rights Education, Advice and Assistance (CHREAA) judicial review against the 2023 mass detentions remains unheard 2+ years later. Right-to-work arrests are ongoing into Jun 2026, not a closed 2023 episode.

**2026 timeline update, protection & rights:** Inua Advocacy's own press log (accessed Sept 2026) shows a denser, more adversarial 2026 than the original research captured: a security operation/raid specifically targeting human trafficking and child abuse (28 Mar 2026); a contested police auction of refugees' seized shipping-container property (11–12 Mar 2026) — a new, distinct property-rights concern, following an earlier partial win where a court ordered the release of refugees' confiscated property (17 Oct 2025); a "Child Sexual Exploitation Rocks Dzaleka" report (31 Jan 2026); and a specific missing-person/suspected-abduction case (young Congolese man, 23 Jul 2026, per SOS Médias Burundi). On the accountability side: 10 police officers were jailed for corruption and aiding human trafficking (Jul 2025) and separate officers were arrested over theft at the camp (Nov 2025) — some enforcement capacity persists despite the broader drawdown. A coalition of NGOs formally challenged the government on rights failures (5 Nov 2025). Dowa Police and Inua Advocacy also formalised a security partnership (Jul 2025), which appears to be the operational channel behind the March 2026 raid. [Inua Advocacy press log](https://inuaadvocacy.org/press)

**Mechanistic link to funding (Pathway B):** UNHCR drawdown → fewer guards/oversight → increased trafficking exposure and youth turning to petty crime — explicitly traced in first-person humanitarian reporting, not inferred. A structural civic-participation counterweight exists: the Dzaleka Children's Parliament (est. 2015, 75 members) is an ongoing self-advocacy structure.

## 6. Malawi political context

Peter Mutharika (DPP) took office 4 Oct 2025 after a landslide win. No presidential statement specifically on refugee policy, Dzaleka, or the Art. 26 reservation has been found — the Mar 2026 UPR rejection is his government's clearest signal so far, but pre-2025 rhetoric should be treated as historical baseline, not a current-government characterization. **Updated 5 Oct 2026:** at the official level, the government's Commissioner for Refugees (Ignacio Maulana, Ministry of Homeland Security) is now on record. He said the end of WFP support means conditions "will get worse", that farming inside the camp cannot sustain refugees, and that the Chitipa settlement awaits compensation funds ([Nation Online, Sept 2026](https://mwnation.com/japan-invests-in-dzaleka-refugees-resilience)). The March 2023 encampment directive remains in force, while the Homeland Security Minister has signalled a possible shift toward citizenship for some long-staying refugees (below).

**Domestic reform continues on its own track.** The Law Commission's Refugees Act review is active and UNHCR-backed (Validation Workshop, 11 Feb 2026; draft bill announced for publication on 9 Oct 2026 — updated 5 Oct 2026, see §2) — running in parallel with, not defunded by, the government's formal UN-level rejection. This reads as a sovereignty distinction: Malawi appears willing to reform its own statute domestically while declining binding international commitments on the same substance.

**Public opinion:** Afrobarometer has a dedicated refugee-attitude question (Q89a); a general "welcome foreigners as neighbours" figure of 77% was found, but the refugee-specific topline has not yet been isolated — a solvable data-pull, not a missing-data gap. No opposition-party position on refugee policy was found, before or after the election.

**Update, Sept 2026:** President Mutharika delivered his first State of the Nation Address on 13 Feb 2026 and a further fiscal-year address around Aug 2026, both framed around "discipline-first" economic recovery and administrative austerity — no refugee- or Dzaleka-specific content was found in coverage of either speech, reinforcing rather than closing the existing gap. Malawi's refugee community itself proactively engaged the new administration: the Urban Leadership of the Refugee Community in Malawi issued a congratulatory statement after Mutharika's Sept 2025 win, explicitly asking the new government to prioritise "rule of law, social and economic development, peace, unity and dignity" — a documented instance of refugee-led civic engagement with the incoming government, distinct from (and more direct than) the Dzaleka Children's Parliament cited in Pathway D.

**Minister signals a possible reversal (26 Jul 2026):** Minister of Homeland Security **Peter Mukhito** said the current financing model for the camp "may no longer be sustainable" and that the government is **considering integrating some refugees into communities by granting citizenship to some long-staying residents**. The Nation called it a "dramatic reversal" of the 2023 "back to camp" operation. The paper also cites 18,000 households and ~64,000 people in the camp ([Nation Online, 26 Jul 2026](https://mwnation.com/hunger-driving-crime-at-dzaleka-refugee-camp)). This is the clearest current-government signal on refugee policy found. **Corrected (audit 5 Oct 2026):** an earlier version said the government "is pursuing" a policy of moving refugees back into Dzaleka in 2026. The sources support only that the March 2023 encampment directive remains in force; The New Humanitarian (30 Sept 2026) recalls UNHCR's warning against it. On 5 Oct 2026 HRDC criticised the Mutharika government, citing among other things the alleged police and immigration links to a smuggling syndicate at Dzaleka ([Malawi24, 5 Oct 2026](https://malawi24.com/2026/10/05/hrdc-trashes-mutharika)).

No Malawi-specific breakdown of World Bank/International Monetary Fund (IMF) program conditionality tied to refugee/migration policy was found, and no donor reaction specifically to the Mar 2026 UPR rejection was found (the EU was present and optimistic at the Aug 2025 NIP launch — whether that posture held is unconfirmed).

## 8. Length of stay and the three durable solutions (added 5 Oct 2026)

UNHCR frames three "durable solutions" for refugees: **voluntary repatriation** (returning home), **resettlement** (moving to a third country), and **local integration** (becoming part of the host country). This section looks at how long people stay in Dzaleka, and at where each of the three solutions stands for Malawi.

### 8.1 How long do people stay?

**No official figure for average length of stay in Dzaleka was found.** Neither UNHCR's public data nor any survey checked reports years-in-camp for current residents. The best available indicators all point to long, often multigenerational stays:

| Indicator | Value | Date | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Age of the camp | Open since 1994, so **32 years** | 2026 | UNHCR (§1) |
| Longest-staying residents | "Some camp residents have been refugees for as long as 28 years" | 2024 | [Journal of Folklore and Education — Dzaleka Art Project](https://jfepublications.org/article/the-dzaleka-art-project) ⚠️ secondary |

A data point worth requesting from UNHCR or the Department of Refugees is the distribution of residents by year of arrival, which the registration database holds but does not publish.

### 8.2 Voluntary repatriation

| Indicator | Value | Date | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| People who voluntarily repatriated | **135** (mainly Burundi, Rwanda, Somalia) | 2025 | [UNHCR Annual Results Report 2025 – Malawi](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/unhcr-annual-results-report-2025-malawi.pdf) ✅ |

**Status: possible for a few, closed for the majority.** About 65% of residents are from the DRC, where the conflict with M23 continues and 6 of 8 peace protocols are unimplemented (§7). Burundi returns are growing and UNHCR now promotes them, but there are well-documented reports of coercion in Tanzania (§7). UNHCR describes the 2025 increase as reflecting "improved access to reliable information". At 135 people, it is 0.2% of the population.

### 8.3 Resettlement and complementary pathways

| Indicator | Baseline | Target 2025 | Actual 2025 | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Refugees submitted by UNHCR for resettlement | 2,430 | 3,000 | **499** (−79% vs. baseline) | [UNHCR Annual Results Report 2025 – Malawi](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/unhcr-annual-results-report-2025-malawi.pdf) ✅ |
| Refugees who departed for resettlement | — | — | **634** (submitted in earlier years) | Same |
| People admitted through complementary pathways (e.g. education, labour) | 43 | 100 | **6** (all education pathways) | Same |

**Status: shrinking fast.** Historically, refugees from Malawi have been resettled to the United States, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Finland and the Netherlands (§8.6). The 2025 submissions are a fifth of the baseline, so departures in coming years are likely to fall too; the 634 departures in 2025 reflect earlier submissions. The main drivers are external:
- the **United States**, historically the largest resettlement country, suspended its refugee admissions program by executive order in January 2025 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12813) ✅). Dzaleka residents describe the news spreading through the camp ([The New Humanitarian, Oct 2025](https://www.thenewhumanitarian.org/opinion/first-person/2025/10/20/remember-we-exist-refugees-malawi-aid-slashed));
- Canada's Economic Mobility Pathways Pilot (EMPP) is "indefinitely paused" ([Border Monitor, 3 Oct 2026](https://bordermonitor.org/general/aid-cuts-deepen-hardship-for-refugees-in-malawi) ⚠️);
- globally, about 37,000 refugees departed through UNHCR-assisted resettlement in 2025, down from more than 116,000 in 2024 (TRT Afrika, citing UNHCR ⚠️ — confirm against UNHCR's Projected Global Resettlement Needs 2027).

### 8.4 Local integration

**Status: legally blocked.**
- **Naturalisation:** Malawi holds a reservation to **Article 34** of the 1951 Convention, so it grants refugees no easier naturalisation than other foreigners. Applications by refugees "are invariably refused, often on the grounds that they are in a position to return to their countries of origin" ([Malawi Refugee Law Reader, AfricanLII](https://africanlii.org/compare?uri-a=%2Fakn%2Fmw%2Fdoc%2Fbook%2F2023-11-01%2Fmalawi-refugee-law-reader%2Feng%402025-01-01&uri-b=%2Fakn%2Fna%2Fdoc%2Fbook%2F2024-01-16%2Fnamibia-refugee-law-reader%2Feng%402025-01-01&portion-a=h1_7&portion-b=h1_7)). Isolated cases have been granted, and some refugees obtained citizenship through marriage ([Refugee Studies Centre, 2010](https://www.rsc.ox.ac.uk/files/files-1/dp-rsd-malawi-2010.pdf)).
- **Encampment** (reservation to Art. 26, the 1989 Refugees Act) bars living and working outside the camp. In March 2023 the government ordered refugees living outside Dzaleka to return; that directive remains in force (§2, §6).
- **The government declined** the UPR recommendations to lift reservations and end encampment (Mar 2026, §2).
- **Two openings:** the draft Refugee Bill (due 9 Oct 2026, §2), and the Homeland Security Minister's statement (26 Jul 2026) that the government is considering citizenship for some long-staying refugees ([Nation Online, 26 Jul 2026](https://mwnation.com/hunger-driving-crime-at-dzaleka-refugee-camp)). UNHCR's own 2023–2026 strategy lists local integration as a goal alongside the "progressive transformation of camps into settlements" ([UNHCR Malawi](https://www.unhcr.org/where-we-work/countries/malawi) ✅).
- **Partial economic inclusion despite the legal block:** 94.6% of refugees and asylum-seekers have a bank or mobile-money account (2025, up from 51.2%; [UNHCR Annual Results Report 2025 – Malawi](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/2026-06/unhcr-annual-results-report-2025-malawi.pdf) ✅). Financial inclusion is advancing even though the right to work is not.

### 8.5 Summary

| Solution | Status | Scale in 2025 | Main barrier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Voluntary repatriation | Open for a few nationalities | 135 people | Ongoing conflict in the DRC (65% of residents); coercion concerns around Burundi returns |
| Resettlement | Shrinking fast | 634 departures; submissions down 79% | US suspension; global quota collapse; Canada's EMPP paused |
| Local integration | Legally blocked | ~0 | Reservations to Arts. 26 and 34; encampment; refused naturalisation |

**Reading:** all three durable solutions are narrowing at the same time as assistance is ending (§3). Most of the ~63,000 residents have no realistic exit. That makes **local integration**, the only one of the three that Malawi itself controls, more central than it looked when it was treated as one legal-pathway item among others. Two signals point there: the draft **Refugee Bill** (9 Oct 2026) and the Homeland Security Minister's statement (26 Jul 2026) that citizenship for some long-staying refugees is being considered.

**Draft problem statement (revised 5 Oct 2026):** Dzaleka's encampment policy locks a rapidly growing refugee population (\~63,000, \~525% of design capacity) into a single site from which **almost no one leaves**: 775 exits through all three durable solutions in 2025 against \~3,600 arrivals, with resettlement collapsing after the US suspension and local integration legally blocked (§8). At the same time, a structural collapse in international financing has ended blanket food assistance (Sept 2026) and withdrawn the protection and disaster-preparedness capacity needed to manage that concentration risk. Malawi has no inter-agency response plan and no 2026 UNHCR funding update, and it is excluded from concessional refugee financing. The result compounds protection harms (GBV, trafficking, crime, reported deaths and disappearances) and disaster exposure (fire, flood, disease) for a population that is effectively permanent.

**Candidate levers (revised 5 Oct 2026):**
1. **Funding remains the pivot node.** It independently degrades the protection pathway and, via overcrowding lock-in, the disaster-risk pathway, and it stalls the one decongestion fix (Kayilizi/Chitipa). Its underlying cause (the reservations, especially Art. 26) appears to carry a direct cost in foregone World Bank financing. The cross-case comparison (`docs/comparison.md` §7) adds a nuance: funding collapse is a pivot in all five camps studied, so "more money" in general is not neglected. What *is* specifically neglected in Malawi is **funding visibility**: no inter-agency plan, no sector breakdown, no 2026 update.
2. **The legal window is now time-bound and more central.** With repatriation and resettlement both closing, local integration is the only route that could convert "permanent temporary" residents into self-reliant ones (right to work, freedom of movement, a path to citizenship). Two concrete openings exist: the Refugee Bill (draft due 9 Oct 2026), and the Homeland Security Minister's 26 Jul 2026 statement that citizenship for some long-staying refugees is under consideration. The Kenya case shows that passing a law is not the same as implementing it, so any engagement should include monitoring of practice.
3. **The disaster-activation interface** (DoDMA ↔ UNHCR) is a defined, buildable gap. A working template exists (the Cox's Bazar relocation protocol, `docs/cases/coxs-bazar.md`). The caveat from that case: a protocol does not prevent deaths when physical risk reduction is unfunded.

## 10. Actor landscape — who is already working here

**No comprehensive, dated NGO partner list exists.** UNHCR's own framing (Jul 2025) describes a shift to a "lighter operational footprint" and "localisation" post-funding-collapse — the standing partner roster (Jesuit Refugee Service (JRS), Churches Action in Relief and Development (CARD), Welthungerhilfe, Baptist Development Service) is UNHCR's general description, not a verified current snapshot. Most under-covered sectors: **child protection** (zero dedicated officers, NGOs "forced to improvise" systems that collapse with each funding cut), then **GBV** (explicitly "scaled back" after UNHCR's funding cuts; UNHCR's GBV line was 21% funded in 2025).

**Civil society:** INUA Advocacy (independent camp monitor, expelled 2023) regained/maintained camp presence — its 2026 crisis is a funding scale-down (Lilongwe office closing Jun 2026, Dzaleka office kept), not renewed government exclusion. No think tank "owns" Dzaleka as a research focus; the most-used single source across this entire research effort turned out to be a refugee-run community archive (195+ documents), not a formal institution.

**Private sector — thin and mostly historical.** The two named corporate partnerships (Microsoft/HP's AppFactory, MyBucks Bank as WFP's cash provider) both date to 2014–2019, no confirmed continuation. **Malawi is entirely absent from the refugee-lens impact-investing field's own literature** (Kiva Refugee Investment Fund, Refugee Investment Facility, Acumen, Global Impact Investing Network (GIIN)) — consistent with the World Bank IDA exclusion (§2), likely the same "adequate protection framework" gate filtering out both public and private capital. One counter-cyclical exception: a small US nonprofit (Global Catalyst/GLOW) tripled its Dzaleka investment in 2026 in direct response to the collapse. **New bilateral donor (updated 5 Oct 2026):** Japan committed **$246,666**: $140,000 for water and sanitation (~30,000 people, camp and host community) and $106,666 for a bio-fertiliser unit run by refugee women's organisation Solidarity of Refugee Women for Social Welfare (SOFERES) with UN Women (300 women, until Feb 2027). [Nation Online, Sept 2026](https://mwnation.com/japan-invests-in-dzaleka-refugees-resilience); [MBC, Sept 2026](https://mbc.mw/japan-funds-biofertiliser-production-for-300-refugee-women-at-dzaleka)

**Philanthropy/think tanks — structurally neglected, not just under-resourced.** Malawi has no UN-coordinated response plan (re-confirmed 5 Oct 2026: OCHA FTS lists none for 2026), so there is no headline appeal drawing in secondary funders. None of the major global refugee philanthropies (IKEA Foundation, Open Society, Gates) or migration think tanks (ODI, Center for Global Development (CGD), Crisis Group, Chatham House, Mixed Migration Centre) show confirmed direct engagement. Smaller/local actors fill part of the gap (Segal Family Foundation, Mastercard Foundation/World University Service of Canada (WUSC)'s DREEM pilot: 57 youth jobs/businesses from a 6-month program). Even the camp's own flagship resilience program — **Tumaini Festival**, a $150k+/yr revenue engine for 2,335+ refugee entrepreneurs since 2014 — faces real cancellation risk in 2026 for lack of corporate sponsorship.

**Correction and update:** the 11th edition of the festival took place 30 Oct–2 Nov 2025 ([Dzaleka Online, 2025 review](https://www.dzaleka.com/2025/12/dzaleka-refugee-camp-2025-year-in-review.html)); whether a 2026 edition goes ahead is not confirmed (the earlier "proceeding in 2026, 11th edition" wording mixed up the years (audit 5 Oct 2026)). The "$150k+/yr" and "2,335+ entrepreneurs" figures above could not be re-sourced in this audit ⚠️. More significantly: **Tumaini Letu won a 2026 Ockenden International Prize**, one of five winners selected from 167 entries across 43 countries and territories ([Ockenden International](https://www.ockendenprizes.org/2026-prizes)) — the most significant positive external validation found anywhere in this research pass, and a concrete counterpoint to Cluster F's "structurally neglected" finding for at least this one initiative. Separately, Inua Advocacy's civil-society activity looks more sustained through 2026 than "shoestring survival" alone suggests: it signed a memorandum of understanding (MoU) with the Media Institute of Southern Africa (MISA) Malawi to amplify refugee voices (9 Oct 2025), formalised a security partnership with Dowa Police (13 Jul 2025) that appears to be the operational channel behind the Mar 2026 anti-trafficking raid (§5), and joined a coalition of NGOs formally challenging the government on rights failures (5 Nov 2025).

## 11. Family & vulnerable groups

**Family structure is being actively reshaped by policy, not just economics.** Camp administration is forcibly consolidating extended families — traditionally spread across multiple houses on one plot — into one house per family card. Households average 5.1 members vs. 4.2 nationally; the double-orphan rate is \~3x the national rate (\~6% vs \~2%) — both figures predate the 2025 cuts, likely understating current pressure.

**Orphans/unaccompanied children:** no formal foster system exists in practice; funding cuts are now driving families to **refuse** to foster unaccompanied children outright, not just under-resourcing existing placements. The Dec 2025 registered count (67) has not been revised as of the latest reporting found, and no functioning Best Interest Determination process is confirmed.

**Religious leadership:** thinly documented — only \~3.4% of the camp's services directory is faith-based; JRS is the one confirmed faith-based operational partner. No source describes religious leaders' role in governance, dispute resolution, GBV referral, or early marriage — a genuine gap, not confirmed absence. What is documented: care for orphans, widows, and single mothers is functionally outsourced to religious networks (a 42-church union) rather than formal child-protection systems.

**Women-headed households:** an employment gap of 48% (married women working in the past year) vs. 84% (married men) — pre-cuts data, likely wider now. Faith-based networks (IAFR, HOPE International) are the primary institutional channel explicitly naming single mothers/widows as a served group. **No source anywhere gives a prevalence figure for female-headed households at Dzaleka** — a real, unfilled data gap, not just an under-reported one.

## 12. Health

Across chronic disease, disability, and mental health, the same pattern repeats: **the problem isn't only service gaps — it's data gaps.** No population-level prevalence figure exists for any of the three, for a population of \~63,000.

**Chronic/non-communicable disease:** no Dzaleka-specific prevalence data exists anywhere (diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic respiratory, cancer). But the funding collapse's effect is directly evidenced: as of Jun 2025, UNHCR-supported clinics ran out of medicine — diabetic/hypertensive patients lost treatment when partner hospitals stopped accepting referrals. A second, independent data point from **18 Sep 2026** shows refugees still denouncing medicine shortages — the same unresolved problem 15 months later, not a one-off spike.

**New data point, Sept 2026:** a 2025 food-security and dietary-diversity survey of 300 women at Dzaleka found 91% experiencing severe food insecurity, with women eating only 2–3 food groups on the previous day on average — adjacent to, not a substitute for, the still-missing 2025/26 MUAC/GAM data flagged above, but the most current population-level nutrition-adjacent figure found. Partial counter-note: the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) Malawi (Feb 2026) reports Dzaleka's own Expanded Programme on Immunisation team has improved vaccine-stock forecasting and reduced stock-outs through dedicated "zero-dose" trainings — one specific, documented instance of a health system holding or improving capacity despite the broader funding collapse, serving a stated catchment of \~16,675 people at that clinic (a separate, larger figure of 70,000+ appears elsewhere for the main Dzaleka Health Centre's total combined refugee+host catchment).

**Disability (PCD):** the only concrete beneficiary numbers found anywhere (Tereska Foundation: 260 children; Parents Association of Children with Disability: 146 members) date to \~2017, when the camp held \~31,000 people — roughly half today's population. Every other named actor is advocacy-only or publishes no beneficiary/funding data. Humanity & Inclusion, the sector's usual global operator, has no confirmed Dzaleka presence.

**Mental health:** the one peer-reviewed prevalence study ([Damiano et al., *Psychiatry Research*, 2022, n=102](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36037741)) found **78% probable depression, 53% with thoughts about death, 25.5% with suicidal plans, 15% with a suicide attempt in the prior 12 months**. Note (audit 5 Oct 2026): it was a screening with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) of 102 people, not a census, and two of its five authors are affiliated with Fraternidade Sem Fronteiras, so it should not be described as fully independent. The findings are alarming, and they are the *baseline*, predating the 2025–26 funding collapse; current severity is unknown. A more dramatic 82% post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) figure traces to a single self-reported mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) provider program pitch — flagged as non-independent, not treated as fact.

**Reading — do the Sept 2026 recommendations still hold? Mostly yes, with three shifts.**

- **B, C, E and G still score best** on all three criteria together, so the core recommendation stands.
  - **B** is now even more clearly neglected. Its most tractable form is *funding visibility* rather than fundraising in general.
  - **C** has become more tractable thanks to the Cox's Bazar template. Its leverage should be read with the caveat above, and the mid-December rains make it time-sensitive.
  - **E** widens from health to "data blackout": length of stay, solutions and food-aid exclusion are all unmeasured.
  - **G** remains the one with an existing operational partner (FSF's Ubuntu Nation) and a live UN request about a PCD clinic.
- **Shift 1 — A moves up.** It is no longer just a "root cause outsiders can't touch". With every durable solution closing, the 9 Oct bill is a time-bound window, and the minister's July statement suggests the government itself is reconsidering encampment for fiscal reasons. The realistic role is analysis and implementation monitoring, not leading the reform.
- **Shift 2 — D becomes more urgent but no more tractable.** Neglect rises to "very high" (no child-protection budget line), and the security signals worsen. It still needs sustained programme capacity, so it remains a strong *awareness* angle more than a near-term intervention.
- **Shift 3 — Dzaleka's case for focus is stronger.** The cross-case comparison confirms that Malawi has the lowest UNHCR funding of the five camps, no inter-agency plan, no 2026 funding update, and no child-protection budget line. That supports keeping Dzaleka as the focus case. **Still do not narrow to a single lever yet**; the bill (9 Oct) and the targeted-distribution outcome (Oct) are two results worth waiting for.

**Proposed focus (5 Oct 2026, for discussion with the professor): cluster G, with C as plan B.**

- **Who acts:** the intended intervention is an open tool, free for anyone, with Fraternidade Sem Fronteiras (FSF) as its first user. FSF already operates the Ubuntu Nation project next to the camp, which makes G the only cluster where the team is already an actor.
- **Research level (cluster G):** how to keep essential services running when they come to depend on local, volunteer-based actors (churches, refugee-led organisations, small NGOs) after the agencies withdraw. The pattern is not FSF-specific:
  - UNHCR-supported clinics ran out of medicine in Jun 2025;
  - refugees still reported medicine shortages in Sept 2026;
  - Inua Advocacy closed its Lilongwe office for lack of funds.
- **Case level (FSF clinic, illustration and possible pilot, not the research object):**
  - the clinic does asynchronous telemedicine: patient photos are reviewed by doctors in Brazil, and the patient likely returns another day for the diagnosis;
  - medicines arrive only as donations carried by volunteer caravans, roughly every 3 months, with 8–30 volunteers and up to 3 kg each — **24–90 kg per caravan at most**, so supply can vary ~4x independent of demand;
  - stock is tracked in a spreadsheet;
  - local purchase is not an option: no stock in the region, per a team member's site visit.

  Source: the team's own knowledge of FSF operations, not yet documented publicly.
- **Design principle:** start from how a refugee asks for help (trusted local focal points, in person or WhatsApp/SMS), let the refugee choose where a case goes, and let only aggregate data leave the tool.
- **First step:** a short, ethical field mapping of how refugees seek help today and of the clinic's baseline (volume, time to diagnosis, return rate, stock-out days).
- **Plan B:** cluster C (emergency coordination), more general and not dependent on FSF, with a pilot before the mid-Dec rains.

**Awareness-specific angles, drawn from the verified material above:**

1. **"The invisible camp"** — Dzaleka has no UN-coordinated response plan, is excluded from World Bank financing, invisible to impact investors, unclaimed by any think tank. A structural-neglect story with a hard financial number attached ($2–2.4B facility, ~$85M Kayilizi financing gap, UNHCR Malawi funds down from $8.4M to $6.0M in a year). Updated 5 Oct 2026: UNHCR Malawi was 22.8% funded in 2025, the lowest of five camps compared, with no 2026 funding update published.
2. **"Nobody is counting them"** — the health-data blackout (chronic disease, disability, mental health) as a story in itself: \~63,000 people, zero prevalence data, one 2022 study showing 78% probable depression before things got worse.
3. **"Families being reshaped by policy"** — the forced housing-consolidation finding, a concrete, human, verifiable rights angle distinct from the more abstract funding-collapse narrative.
4. **"Who's actually there"** — a positive-framing counter-narrative: local/faith-based/refugee-led actors (churches, INUA Advocacy, Tumaini Festival, Ubuntu Nation) sustaining the camp while formal institutions withdraw — useful for a hope-forward awareness angle rather than crisis-only framing.
5. **"No way out"** (new, 5 Oct 2026) — 32 years, three exits, all closing: 1,520 voluntary repatriations of Dzaleka nationalities in 1994–2025, zero recorded naturalisations, and resettlement down 79% after the US suspension. In 2025, 775 people left while \~3,600 arrived. Children are born into refugee status. A concrete, data-backed story about permanence, with two live hooks: the 9 Oct Refugee Bill, and the minister's July 2026 signal on citizenship for long-staying refugees.
