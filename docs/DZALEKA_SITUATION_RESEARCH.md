## 2. Pathway A — Legal & policy

Malawi's encampment policy rests on its reservation to **Article 26 of the 1951 Refugee Convention** (freedom of movement), treated as non-binding. Domestic law is the **Refugees Act, 1989**, which sets status-determination procedure but does not enumerate refugee rights. [Dzaleka encyclopedia](https://services.dzaleka.com/encyclopedia/law-and-encampment-policy)

| Event | Date | Detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mass arrests/detention | 17–18 May 2023 | 505 people (incl. 117 children) detained, forcibly relocated |
| National Implementation Plan on Migration (2025–2029) launched | 29 Aug 2025 | Government + IOM, signaled rights-based shift — launched under the *outgoing* government |
| **Government change** | 24 Sept 2025 | Peter Mutharika (DPP) defeats incumbent Chakwera, 57% vs 33%; sworn in 4 Oct 2025 |
| UN Universal Periodic Review, 50th session | 4 Nov 2025 | 294 recommendations; Malawi supported 228, "noted" (declined) 65 |
| **UPR outcome formally adopted — Malawi declines refugee-specific asks** | 18 Mar 2026 | Confirmed: the 65 declined recommendations include lifting the Art. 26 reservation, modifying the 1989 Act, and ending encampment |
| Law Commission Refugees Act review — National Validation Workshop | 11 Feb 2026 | Active, UNHCR-backed technical-level review; bill expected in Parliament 2026 |

**Reading:** Malawi is pursuing two parallel, seemingly contradictory tracks — a domestic legislative review it controls, and a refusal of the same substance as a binding UN-level commitment. This looks like a sovereignty distinction, not simple inaction.

**Financial consequence, newly identified:** Malawi is excluded from the World Bank's **IDA Window for Host Communities and Refugees** (WHR/RSW) — a $2–2.4B concessional financing facility available to 14 other refugee-hosting countries, including three of Dzaleka's own origin countries (Burundi, DRC, Rwanda). Eligibility requires an "adequate protection framework" per UNHCR advisory opinion, which the Art. 26 reservation likely fails to meet. Not confirmed that Malawi applied and was rejected — this is a strong inference, not a stated fact.

**Decongestion instrument tied to this pathway:** Kayilizi Open Settlement, Chitipa District — 330 hectares, designed as an integration pilot. Estimated cost **US$90 million; stuck at financing stage** as of mid-2026.

## 3. Pathway B — Funding collapse

This is a global structural shock, not a Malawi-specific dip. UNHCR's 2026 Global Appeal needs **US$8.505 billion**; the UN's 2026 hyper-prioritized ask fell to **US$23 billion — the lowest since 2017** — after the US dismantled USAID in 2025. [UNHCR Global Appeal 2026](https://www.unhcr.org/publications/global-appeal-2026)

| Indicator | Value | Date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UNHCR global spend, down 22% vs. 2024 | $3.830B | 2025 |
| UNHCR Malawi requirement | $26.3M | 2025 |
| UNHCR Malawi funded | 12% (Mar) → 18% (Jul) → 23% (Oct) | 2025 |
| **UNHCR Malawi operational budget cut** | **$8M → $1M (-90%)** | 2025→2026 |
| WFP cash transfer, pre-cut → post-cut | \~$10.30 → <$9.00/person/month | 2025 |
| WFP Malawi shortfall to restore full rations | $11M through Dec 2026 | 2026 |

**USAID dismantling, Dzaleka-specific:** per ProPublica-obtained State Dept cables, WFP's Malawi country representative warned in advance that the funding collapse would trigger border closures, protests at UN/WFP offices, and food-aid suspension — before these happened. A named State Dept official (Jeremy Lewin) ordered WFP grant terminations across 14+ countries in Apr 2025 (independently confirmed by AP/Reuters/ABC/PBS) — but **Malawi is not on that named list**, and is also absent from the list of countries whose grants were later reinstated (Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Jordan, Ecuador, Somalia). Dzaleka's own funding collapse (UNHCR-attributed, Jun 2025) looks like a separate, later event, not a direct consequence of the Lewin cuts.

**Current WFP ration status (reconciled, most current source wins):** local press reported rations "cut to zero" in Jun 2026, but WFP's own Country Brief (Jul 2026) confirms rations were still at **60%** as of that report, with a full cut framed as a *future* risk tied to a **September 2026** trigger date — not yet realized as of the latest data found. WFP/UNHCR are jointly considering an unpublished "painful prioritization" triage that would exclude most refugees from food assistance.

**Documented causal chain:** funding cut → UNHCR downsizing (guards/supervisors laid off) → NGO withdrawal (Plan International exited entirely, Oct 2025) → protection-service vacuum → deteriorating protection outcomes (Pathway D).

## 4. Pathway C — Disaster risk & institutional capacity

| Hazard | Assessment | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flood | Credible, elevated — seasonal (Nov–Apr) heavy rains | Terrain/DTM studies exist; no public floodplain delineation |
| Fire | High likelihood/impact — dense shelter layout, flammable materials | Documented 2023–2024 fire incidents; UNHCR after-action report |
| Disease (cholera, malaria) | Actively surveilled, background risk | Not currently an acute outbreak per latest sampling |

**The evacuation-command gap is a real capability gap, not just a documentation gap.** The Multi-Hazard Early Warning System platform (MHEWC.org) states district-level DoDMA still *needs to build* — not just publish — camp-level contingency plans, early-warning triggers, and evacuation protocols. Referral capacity: Dowa District Hospital (\~10km), tertiary referral Lilongwe (\~45km); emergency services rated "critical" — limited ambulances, no night access.

Dowa District was on the affected-councils list for Malawi's Jan–Mar 2026 floods; camp-specific damage at Dzaleka is not yet confirmed either way. **Cyclone Freddy (Mar 2023) impact on Dzaleka is refuted**, not just unconfirmed — a contemporaneous statement says directly: "Freddy has not had any impact on refugees in Dzaleka camp" (Dowa fell outside all 15 officially affected southern districts).

**WASH data exists** (correcting an earlier read that it didn't): water access, latrine ratios, and hygiene-promoter ratios are documented across three time periods. A 2024 malnutrition baseline exists (stunting 38%, wasting 1%) — but it predates the 2025–26 ration cuts, so the nutritional impact of the funding collapse remains undocumented; no 2025/2026 MUAC or GAM data was found.

One risk-financing precedent: 4,000 households received a parametric-insurance payout ($33/household/month) after El Niño-driven crop failures.

## 5. Pathway D — Protection & vulnerable groups

| Indicator | Value / finding | Date |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dedicated child protection officer on-site | None, at Dzaleka or Karonga Reception Center | Dec 2025 |
| Registered unaccompanied/separated children | 67 (likely undercount) | Dec 2025 |
| Reported crime cases, Dowa Police | 34 vs 20 same period prior year (+70%) | Jan–Jul 2026 |
| Trafficking status | UNHCR characterizes Dzaleka as a human trafficking hotspot | 2026 |

**GBV is a recurring structural mechanism, not a one-off 2025–26 shock.** The identical WFP-ration-cut → survival-sex pattern occurred in 2015–16 too, independently named by WFP's own country director, a community leader, Luxembourg's Deputy PM, and Plan International staff. Plan International's Oct 2025 exit removed the only actor holding child protection, SGBV, and human rights under one mandate; no source shows who absorbed that caseload. No camp-wide GBV prevalence survey exists, and no confirmed integrated referral center (One-Stop Centre) serves Dzaleka.

**Broader human-rights context:** INUA Advocacy, an independent camp monitor, was expelled from Dzaleka in Nov 2023 — likely retaliatory. The CHREAA judicial review against the 2023 mass detentions remains unheard 2+ years later. Right-to-work arrests are ongoing into Jun 2026, not a closed 2023 episode.

**Mechanistic link to funding (Pathway B):** UNHCR drawdown → fewer guards/oversight → increased trafficking exposure and youth turning to petty crime — explicitly traced in first-person humanitarian reporting, not inferred. A structural civic-participation counterweight exists: the Dzaleka Children's Parliament (est. 2015, 75 members) is an ongoing self-advocacy structure.

## 6. Malawi political context

Peter Mutharika (DPP) took office 4 Oct 2025 after a landslide win. No confirmed statement from his administration specifically on refugee policy, Dzaleka, or the Art. 26 reservation has been found — the Mar 2026 UPR rejection is his government's clearest signal so far, but pre-2025 rhetoric should be treated as historical baseline, not a current-government characterization.

**Domestic reform continues on its own track.** The Law Commission's Refugees Act review is active and UNHCR-backed (Validation Workshop, 11 Feb 2026; a bill is expected in Parliament in 2026) — running in parallel with, not defunded by, the government's formal UN-level rejection. This reads as a sovereignty distinction: Malawi appears willing to reform its own statute domestically while declining binding international commitments on the same substance.

**Public opinion:** Afrobarometer has a dedicated refugee-attitude question (Q89a); a general "welcome foreigners as neighbours" figure of 77% was found, but the refugee-specific topline has not yet been isolated — a solvable data-pull, not a missing-data gap. No opposition-party position on refugee policy was found, before or after the election.

No Malawi-specific breakdown of World Bank/IMF program conditionality tied to refugee/migration policy was found, and no donor reaction specifically to the Mar 2026 UPR rejection was found (the EU was present and optimistic at the Aug 2025 NIP launch — whether that posture held is unconfirmed).

## 7. Population origins

| Country | Share of Dzaleka population | Current status | Repatriation outlook |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| DRC | \~65% (largest group) | Active conflict; Rwanda continues backing M23 per Jun 2026 UN reporting | Narrow, negotiated returns only — 6 of 8 Doha peace protocols unimplemented |
| Burundi | \~22% (91% of the non-DRC population) | Large, accelerating repatriation push from Tanzania (198,705 + 85,519 returned by Jan 2026, target 3,000/week) | **UNHCR has not declared conditions safe**; independent reporting documents coercion (shelters demolished, people struck from food lists) — some sources call it de facto refoulement |
| Rwanda | \~12% | Regionally implicated as a conflict actor (M23 backing) rather than a return destination in current reporting | Not established as safe for return in this research |
| Mozambique | <1% | Minor presence, not a research focus this pass | — |

**Confirmed secondary effect:** UNHCR Southern Africa confirms 109 Burundians were repatriated directly from Dzaleka via Kamuzu Airport — the Burundi repatriation push is not confined to Tanzania, it reaches into Malawi's own caseload. No source yet connects this to any broader shift in new-arrival composition at Dzaleka.

Monthly new arrivals remain dominated by DRC (62%) and Burundi (19%), suggesting the underlying displacement pressure from both origin countries is ongoing even as repatriation programs run in parallel — a tension not resolved in current reporting.

## 8. Verification, evidence gaps, and draft ToC skeleton

**Claims checked this pass:**

| Claim | Status |
| --- | --- |
| "UNHCR stopped registering new refugees, redirecting to Zambia" | Still unverified — but Dzaleka's UNHCR field office has in fact been closed 3+ months since \~Nov 2025, likely the real event this claim distorted |
| Cyclone Freddy (Mar 2023) hit Dzaleka | Refuted — contemporaneous source states no impact |
| Jeremy Lewin personally ordered WFP grant cuts | Confirmed as a real mechanism (14 countries), but Malawi is not on the named list |
| Burundi repatriation reaches Malawi | Confirmed — 109 repatriated directly from Dzaleka |

**Remaining evidence gaps:** no reconciled population registry by date; no public floodplain/drainage/evacuation-route map; no camp-wide GBV prevalence survey; no 2025/26 malnutrition (MUAC/GAM) data; no confirmed record of Malawi applying to and being rejected from the World Bank's IDA host-refugee financing window; no Mutharika-administration statement specifically on refugee policy.

**Draft problem statement:** Dzaleka's encampment policy locks a rapidly growing refugee population into a single, \~500%-over-capacity site, while a structural collapse in international humanitarian financing simultaneously withdraws the protection and disaster-preparedness capacity needed to manage that concentration risk — compounding both protection harms (GBV, trafficking, crime) and disaster exposure (fire, flood, disease), and excluding Malawi from concessional financing that could otherwise offset the shortfall.

**Candidate high-leverage lever:** funding is the pivot node — it independently degrades the protection pathway and, via overcrowding lock-in, the disaster-risk pathway; it also stalls the one structural fix (Kayilizi decongestion) that would reduce both, and its underlying cause (the Art. 26 reservation) appears to carry a direct, quantifiable cost in foregone World Bank financing.

## 9. Actor landscape — who is already working here

**No comprehensive, dated NGO partner list exists.** UNHCR's own framing (Jul 2025) describes a shift to a "lighter operational footprint" and "localisation" post-funding-collapse — the standing partner roster (JRS, CARD, Welthungerhilfe, Baptist Development Service) is UNHCR's general description, not a verified current snapshot. Most under-covered sectors: **child protection** (zero dedicated officers, NGOs "forced to improvise" systems that collapse with each funding cut), then **GBV** (explicitly "scaled back" after UNHCR's 90% budget cut).

**Civil society:** INUA Advocacy (independent camp monitor, expelled 2023) regained/maintained camp presence — its 2026 crisis is a funding scale-down (Lilongwe office closing Jun 2026, Dzaleka office kept), not renewed government exclusion. No think tank "owns" Dzaleka as a research focus; the most-used single source across this entire research effort turned out to be a refugee-run community archive (195+ documents), not a formal institution.

**Private sector — thin and mostly historical.** The two named corporate partnerships (Microsoft/HP's AppFactory, MyBucks Bank as WFP's cash provider) both date to 2014–2019, no confirmed continuation. **Malawi is entirely absent from the refugee-lens impact-investing field's own literature** (Kiva Refugee Investment Fund, Refugee Investment Facility, Acumen, GIIN) — consistent with the World Bank IDA exclusion (§3), likely the same "adequate protection framework" gate filtering out both public and private capital. One counter-cyclical exception: a small US nonprofit (Global Catalyst/GLOW) tripled its Dzaleka investment in 2026 in direct response to the collapse.

**Philanthropy/think tanks — structurally neglected, not just under-resourced.** Malawi has no UN-coordinated response plan, so there is no headline appeal drawing in secondary funders. None of the major global refugee philanthropies (IKEA Foundation, Open Society, Gates) or migration think tanks (ODI, CGD, Crisis Group, Chatham House, Mixed Migration Centre) show confirmed direct engagement. Smaller/local actors fill part of the gap (Segal Family Foundation, Mastercard Foundation/WUSC's DREEM pilot: 57 youth jobs/businesses from a 6-month program). Even the camp's own flagship resilience program — **Tumaini Festival**, a $150k+/yr revenue engine for 2,335+ refugee entrepreneurs since 2014 — faces real cancellation risk in 2026 for lack of corporate sponsorship.

## 10. Family & vulnerable groups

**Family structure is being actively reshaped by policy, not just economics.** Camp administration is forcibly consolidating extended families — traditionally spread across multiple houses on one plot — into one house per family card. Households average 5.1 members vs. 4.2 nationally; the double-orphan rate is \~3x the national rate (\~6% vs \~2%) — both figures predate the 2025 cuts, likely understating current pressure.

**Orphans/unaccompanied children:** no formal foster system exists in practice; funding cuts are now driving families to **refuse** to foster unaccompanied children outright, not just under-resourcing existing placements. The Dec 2025 registered count (67) has not been revised as of the latest reporting found, and no functioning Best Interest Determination process is confirmed.

**Religious leadership:** thinly documented — only \~3.4% of the camp's services directory is faith-based; JRS (Jesuit Refugee Service) is the one confirmed faith-based operational partner. No source describes religious leaders' role in governance, dispute resolution, GBV referral, or early marriage — a genuine gap, not confirmed absence. What is documented: care for orphans, widows, and single mothers is functionally outsourced to religious networks (a 42-church union) rather than formal child-protection systems.

**Women-headed households:** an employment gap of 48% (married women working in the past year) vs. 84% (married men) — pre-cuts data, likely wider now. Faith-based networks (IAFR, HOPE International) are the primary institutional channel explicitly naming single mothers/widows as a served group. **No source anywhere gives a prevalence figure for female-headed households at Dzaleka** — a real, unfilled data gap, not just an under-reported one.

## 11. Health

Across chronic disease, disability, and mental health, the same pattern repeats: **the problem isn't only service gaps — it's data gaps.** No population-level prevalence figure exists for any of the three, for a population of 62,000+.

**Chronic/non-communicable disease:** no Dzaleka-specific prevalence data exists anywhere (diabetes, hypertension, CVD, chronic respiratory, cancer). But the funding collapse's effect is directly evidenced: as of Jun 2025, UNHCR-supported clinics ran out of medicine — diabetic/hypertensive patients lost treatment when partner hospitals stopped accepting referrals. A second, independent data point from **18 Sep 2026** shows refugees still denouncing medicine shortages — the same unresolved problem 15 months later, not a one-off spike.

**Disability (PCD):** the only concrete beneficiary numbers found anywhere (Tereska Foundation: 260 children; Parents Association of Children with Disability: 146 members) date to \~2017, when the camp held \~31,000 people — roughly half today's population. Every other named actor is advocacy-only or publishes no beneficiary/funding data. Humanity & Inclusion, the sector's usual global operator, has no confirmed Dzaleka presence.

**Mental health:** the one independently-published prevalence study (Damiano et al., *Psychiatry Research*, 2022, n=102) found **78% probable depression, 25.5% with suicidal plans, 15% with a suicide attempt in the prior 12 months** — alarming, and that's the *baseline*, predating the 2025–26 funding collapse; current severity is unknown. A more dramatic 82% PTSD figure traces to a single self-reported MHPSS-provider program pitch — flagged as non-independent, not treated as fact.

C (disaster/institutional capacity) is the one with direct technical continuity to this repo's existing ERCF/RICS work. G (local/faith-based response, including FSF's own Ubuntu Nation project) is the one with an existing operational partner and, per this conversation, a live UN request specifically about a PCD clinic — details still being gathered.

**Awareness-specific angles, drawn from the verified material above:**

1. **"The invisible camp"** — Dzaleka has no UN-coordinated response plan, is excluded from World Bank financing, invisible to impact investors, unclaimed by any think tank. A structural-neglect story with a hard financial number attached ($2–2.4B facility, $90M Kayilizi gap, 90% UNHCR budget cut).
2. **"Nobody is counting them"** — the health-data blackout (chronic disease, disability, mental health) as a story in itself: 62,000+ people, zero prevalence data, one 2022 study showing 78% probable depression before things got worse.
3. **"Families being reshaped by policy"** — the forced housing-consolidation finding, a concrete, human, verifiable rights angle distinct from the more abstract funding-collapse narrative.
4. **"Who's actually there"** — a positive-framing counter-narrative: local/faith-based/refugee-led actors (churches, INUA Advocacy, Tumaini Festival, Ubuntu Nation) sustaining the camp while formal institutions withdraw — useful for a hope-forward awareness angle rather than crisis-only framing.
