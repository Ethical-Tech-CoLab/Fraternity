# Cox's Bazar (Kutupalong-Balukhali Expansion Site), Bangladesh — Situation Analysis

Broad-to-narrow situation research for a sector-level Theory of Change (ToC)
covering the Rohingya refugee camps at Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, with primary
focus on the Kutupalong-Balukhali expansion site (the largest single site
within the 33-camp response). Compiled via Tavily web research and search,
September 2026. **Not the ToC itself yet** — this is the evidence base the
ToC's problem tree, pathways, and assumptions should be built from. Follows
the same sourcing discipline as `dzaleka.md`: every figure keeps its
original source, date, and reporting period; nothing is silently reconciled
across conflicting numbers.

A note on the numbers: population figures for "Bangladesh Rohingya total"
diverge across sources and dates even within 2026 — 1,184,864 (28 Feb 2026,
per UNHCR/RefugePoint), 1,200,171 (Jun 2026, UNHCR dashboard PDF), 1,202,034
(31 Jul 2026, UNHCR Bangladesh). These are **not contradictions** — the
population is genuinely growing by ~15–20,000/month from new arrivals plus
births, so the "current" figure is only ever a snapshot. Monsoon-disaster
casualty figures for July 2026 diverge much more sharply across sources
reporting on the *same* event window (10 vs. 15 vs. 17 deaths) because they
were issued at different hours/days as the event was still unfolding and use
different counting scopes (Rohingya-only vs. district-wide, point-in-time vs.
cumulative). Always check the date and scope attached to a figure before
using it.

## 2. Pathway A — Legal / policy

**Root structural driver.** Bangladesh is **not a signatory** to the 1951
Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol and has no domestic refugee/asylum
law. Rohingya are governed as "foreigners" under the colonial-era
**Foreigners Act, 1946**, which the government has used to confine them to
designated camps. The government does not use the term "refugee" — Rohingya
are officially designated **"Forcibly Displaced Myanmar Nationals" (FDMN)**,
a term chosen partly to avoid triggering obligations or precedent associated
with refugee status. [EJIL:Talk!](https://www.ejiltalk.org/evaluating-bangladeshs-legal-framework-for-rohingya-refugees-gaps-and-solutions);
[ODI HPN 2018](https://odihpn.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/HE-73_web.pdf);
[IOM Bangladesh Crisis Response Plan 2026](https://crisisresponse.iom.int/response/bangladesh-crisis-response-plan-2026)

**Camp governance/legal-administrative structure (as of Aug 2026):** the
**Refugee Relief and Repatriation Commissioner (RRRC)**, under the Ministry
of Disaster Management and Relief (MoDMR), holds overall camp management
authority — 1 RRRC, 1 Additional RRRC, 6 Assistant RRRC, 22 Camp-in-Charge
(CiC), 8 Assistant CiC, and ~350 UNHCR-funded support staff. [RRRC "At a
Glance," Aug
2026](https://objectstorage.ap-dcc-gazipur-1.oraclecloud15.com/n/axvjbnqprylg/b/V2Ministry/o/office-rrrc/2026/8/9a85a093-e0f6-4a0f-8293-b7a042ccc7bc.pdf)

**Right to work and freedom of movement:** neither is formally granted.
Rohingya who work outside camps (informally, in Cox's Bazar city, hotels,
markets, boats) do so unregistered and at depressed wages; periodic arrests
and returns to camp are documented (e.g., 8 Rohingya detained in Bandarban,
May 2025). Movement restrictions include checkpoints, curfews, and — per a
long-standing 2022 HRW report — increasing harassment relative to earlier
years. [Griffith Journal of Law & Human Dignity](https://griffithlawjournal.org/index.php/gjlhd/article/view/986);
[EUAA Country Focus, 2025](https://www.euaa.europa.eu/coi/bangladesh/2025/country-focus/82-ethnic-and-religious-minorities/823-rohingyas);
[HRW, 2022](https://www.hrw.org/news/2022/04/04/bangladesh-new-restrictions-rohingya-camps)

**Reform target, structurally identified across sources:** Bangladesh's
policy has historically prioritized near-term repatriation over long-term
planning, explicitly to avoid signaling acceptance of a protracted stay
(which analysts argue would reduce pressure on Myanmar and could create a
"pull factor"). International Crisis Group and others argue this posture is
now counterproductive and self-undermining, given repatriation's
demonstrated non-viability. [Crisis Group, "A Sustainable Policy for
Rohingya Refugees in
Bangladesh"](https://www.crisisgroup.org/rpt/asia-pacific/bangladesh/303-sustainable-policy-rohingya-refugees-bangladesh)

## 4. Pathway C — Disaster / risk exposure

*This is the pathway with the most real weight at this site: the camps sit
on deforested, steep hillsides (100–250m) in one of Bangladesh's most
disaster-prone districts, during an intense monsoon season (~80% of
Bangladesh's annual rainfall falls June–October) and within the Bay of
Bengal cyclone belt.*

| Hazard | Likelihood / impact assessment | Key evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Landslide | Very high, recurring annually | Deforestation for camp construction (2,283 ha lost 2016–17 in Kutupalong-Balukhali alone) removed root structures stabilizing slopes; UNHCR/IOM estimates: 100,000+ refugees exposed to "grave danger," 85,000+ could lose shelters, 23,000+ on steep slopes at direct landslide risk; IOM separately cites 31,000+ in high-risk flood/landslide areas | [MDPI 2018](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-4292/10/5/689); [ImpACT International](https://impactpolicies.org/news/991/bangladeshs-rohingya-camps-face-recurring-monsoon-disaster-risks) |

## 6. Cross-pathway causal skeleton

```
[Bangladesh non-signatory to 1951 Convention; FDMN status,
 not refugee status; encampment under Foreigners Act 1946] ──┬──→ [No right to work / restricted movement] ──→ [95% aid-dependence] ──→ [extreme exposure to any funding shock]
                                                               │
                                                               └──→ [Repatriation-first policy blocks durable-solution planning] ──→ [Protracted overcrowding locked in]
                                                                                                                                              │
                                                                                                                                              ▼
[Funding collapse: JRP funded 46% (2025), 50% (2026);        [Deforested, hilly, monsoon/cyclone-belt siting] ──→ [Landslide/flood risk: 85,000+ shelter-loss exposure,
 declining trend since 2017; WFP rations cut 3x since 2023]       23,000+ direct landslide exposure]
        │                                                                          │
        ├──→ [Protection sector worst-funded (24%)] ──→ [Armed-group (ARSA/RSO) control of many camps] ──→ [killings, extortion, forced recruitment, trafficking]
        │                                                          │
        │                                                          └──→ [desperation-driven sea crossings] ──→ [~900 dead/missing, 2025 — deadliest route on earth]
        │
        └──→ [Shelter remains bamboo/tarpaulin (228,102 units)] ──→ [amplifies both fire risk AND landslide/wind vulnerability]

[New arrivals: 130,000 (2025) + 35,000 (2026 est.), driven by
 Rakhine conflict Bangladesh cannot control] ──→ [population growth outpaces any funding or decongestion plan] ──→ [density keeps intensifying across all four pathways]
```

**Reading:** unlike Dzaleka, where funding collapse was the clean pivot
node, Cox's Bazar has **two entangled pivots**: (1) the legal/policy
choice to treat the population as temporarily "displaced" rather than
durably present, which blocks investment in durable shelter, livelihoods,
and decongestion even when funding exists; and (2) the funding collapse
itself, which independently degrades protection (Pathway D) and — via
delayed drainage/slope work and continued reliance on flammable, unstable
shelter materials — disaster resilience (Pathway C). The disaster pathway
here is not merely a consequence of the other three, as at Dzaleka — it is
also a **direct physical constraint on the site itself** (steep, deforested,
monsoon/cyclone-exposed terrain) that would carry serious risk even under a
fully-funded, rights-respecting policy regime. A new-arrival inflow that
Bangladesh does not control (driven by conflict inside Myanmar) is a fifth,
exogenous pressure variable that intensifies all four pathways
simultaneously and does not appear in the Dzaleka skeleton in comparable
form.

## 8. Draft ToC skeleton (for review, not final)

**Problem statement (draft):** Bangladesh's non-recognition of Rohingya
refugee status and its repatriation-first policy posture have locked over
1.2 million people into a small, deforested, hazard-prone strip of land
without the right to work or move freely — while a structural, multi-year
decline in international humanitarian financing (now compounded by the
2025–26 global aid-funding shock) is simultaneously eroding the protection,
shelter, and disaster-preparedness capacity needed to manage a population
that keeps growing due to conflict inside Myanmar that neither Bangladesh
nor the humanitarian community controls.

**Candidate long-term outcome:** Rohingya refugees at Cox's Bazar live in
conditions where legal status, population density/growth, and available
protection/disaster-response capacity are no longer structurally mismatched
— regardless of whether or when repatriation becomes viable.

**Candidate intermediate outcomes (one per pathway):**
1. **Legal (A):** A durable-stay-compatible policy framework (even short of
   formal refugee status) that permits regulated work, freedom of movement,
   and durable shelter materials, without being read as abandoning
   repatriation as the eventual goal.
2. **Financing (B):** A funding base for the JRP that is both larger and
   less concentrated in a single donor (US = 23% of 2025 tracked funding),
   with protection sector funding brought up from its current worst-in-class
   24% coverage.
3. **Disaster risk (C):** Full-response-wide (not subset) cyclone-shelter
   and evacuation-route capacity, published and reconciled activation
   protocols between RRRC/CPP/camp level, and a shift away from
   bamboo/tarpaulin as the default shelter material.
4. **Protection (D):** Restored/expanded protection-sector funding;
   documented, resourced counter-trafficking and GBV case management;
   an addressed pathway for the near-900-deaths-a-year maritime crossing
   crisis that treats it as a protection failure, not just a border-security
   issue.

**Key assumptions to stress-test:**
- That the "durable materials for safer shelters" policy shift referenced
  by the interim government (2025) is more than rhetorical — 228,102
  bamboo/tarpaulin shelters were still standing as of Aug 2026.
- That donor diversification is achievable given Cox's Bazar's lower global
  media/political visibility relative to Gaza, Ukraine, and Sudan in the
  current (2025–26) hyper-prioritized, cut-down global aid environment.
- That reducing armed-group control of the camps (Pathway D) is tractable
  without also addressing the underlying drivers — inadequate camp policing
  presence, lack of legal livelihoods, and extortion opportunities created
  by aid scarcity — rather than treating it as a pure law-enforcement
  problem.
- That the disaster-risk pathway (C) can be meaningfully improved without
  physical decongestion (moving people off the steepest, highest-risk
  slopes) — which in turn requires either more land (politically fraught)
  or a reduction in population growth pressure that is itself driven by a
  Myanmar conflict outside this response's control.

**Suggested next research pass:** a dedicated pull of the RRRC/UNHCR
"Multi-Hazard Emergency Lifesaving Relocation Protocol" and "Landslide Risk
Prevention and Mitigation Measures Strategy & Action Plan" documents
(closes the largest structural gap in Pathway C), cross-referenced against
this same request for Dzaleka to see whether a shared evacuation-protocol
evaluation framework is possible across both cases.
